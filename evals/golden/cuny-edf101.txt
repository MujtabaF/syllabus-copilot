   COURSE DESCRIPTION

The Education First Year Seminar (EDF 101) is required of all new students majoring in Education.
Its goals are to introduce students to the liberal arts, campus culture, and the education field   . The course
also aims to develop a better understanding of the learning process and acquire essential academic
skills. Taught by ELA faculty and supported by Peers Advisors and   co-curricular professionals, this
course addresses issues related to contemporary college life and   to the field of education (birth through
12th Grade).

Entering college marks the beginning of a journey towards greater knowledge, the discovery of a new
identity as a student and future graduate, and the search for career opportunities. This seminar will help
you get started

With  this  exploration.  The  themes  of  this  course  are  many,  but  we  will  especially  focus  on:  (1)
culturally responsive teaching, (2) equality, equity, and social justice, (3) personal and professional
well-being.

We will hold important conversations and discussions on the field of teaching, and what the state of
the profession is, in light of recent social developments as well as   nation-wide issues. Along with these
discussions, we will explore well   -being/mindfulness to keep us grounded, focused, and energized with
positive outlooks. We will explore what mindfulness means and various ways we can practice.

This course is designed to be fully in person.

About Mindfulness

   "Mindfulness" is a word we use to describe a conscious activity that focuses on bringing our
   attention back to ourselves. Our culture and social mores (values) incline us to look for things
   outside of us: to focus on our jobs, careers, and always be busy; to look for immediate sources of
   gratification; to constantly seek entertainment, to glorify celebrities, to focus on what we don't
   have, and to even immerse ourselves in gossip and judgement of others. All of those things are
   normal aspects of life that are easy to be swallowed up in if we are not in total control of our
   actions.

   Mindfulness seeks to bring our attention to ourselves, to attempt to find that control we do not
   have, to ground ourselves on our very existence and to witness the miracle of breathing, of being
   alive, and on establishing a connection with what is good within ourselves and in humanity.
   Mindfulness can be achieved through many activities that will help us practice bringing our
   minds to beautiful, worthy, and flourishing thoughts that would counteract the natural propensity
   we have towards catastrophizing our everyday experiences. Taking a walk, exercising, journal
   writing, practices of gratitude, and meditation are all practices that are not numbing (that help us
   forget something and not "feel bad") but that are rather edifying. Here lies the difference with
   "just getting distracted to forget something." Mindfulness is an active practice of keeping your
   mind full of worthy thoughts that will help you be kind to yourself and to others, that will allow
   you to find satisfaction within yourself.

   While it is hard to stave off stress (stemming from various aspects of life, over which we have
   little to no control), we may condition ourselves to not be completely overwhelmed by it, and to
   respond to stress in constructive ways. Stress is not only a mental state; its way of becoming
   manifested is by shaking up our immune system, sending signals all over our body, cautioning
   that we are in danger. That is when many illnesses raise, telling us that it is time to take
   immediate and urgent care of ourselves. But this care is a practice; it is a marathon. Well-being
   has to be a consistent conscious choice we take every day.

   Our choice in a career in Education comes with a heavy emotional load. Working with children,
   their families, and understanding social and political circumstances that give rise to inequalities
   in academic achievement, can take a toll on us. It is important that we begin practicing available
   ways of grounding ourselves and to find that stability and well-being within ourselves. I hope the
   Education First Year Seminar will be a first step in getting you acquainted with some of these
   practices. And I hope that I can serve as guide and support. Please feel free to reach out if you
   have questions or concerns.

For your convenience, and as a way to explore mindfulness, download the free mindfulness app which we will
be using, found here:  https://www.uclahealth.org/marc/ucla-mindful-app

   COURSE LEARNING GOALS & OBJECTIVES

   Following full participation in EDF101, as an Education Major you will:

   ●   Increase your understanding of the field of education birth through 12th grade; New York State
   Department of Education requirements, including certification and fingerprint requirements;
   the foundational knowledge, key concepts, and habits of mind of the field of education, e.g.
   observation, interpretation, critical thinking, synthesis, analysis, reflection and evaluation.
   ●   Increase your understanding of the purposes of higher education and the policies and
   expectations of LaGuardia Community College and its faculty, including engaging with the
   College through active curricular, advising, and co-curricular participation.
   ●   Familiarize you with the types of research and methods utilized in the field of education and
   how theory influences teaching practices, the concepts of ethics and values related to field of
   education and to reflect on how these values and ethics directly influence personal, academic,
   and professional success in their teaching careers; the structure and logic of academic writing,
   including the close reading of texts; and key academic support resources related to student life
   and engage them in using these resources to solve academic issues related to advising, course
   selection, academic skills, and extracurricular activities.
   ●   Cultivate academic habits of success (e.g. note-taking, active reading, test preparation, and
   collaborative learning skills) and the self-management habits necessary for academic success
   (e.g. time management, motivation, self-responsibility and financial literacy); to situate and
   contextualize learning by making connections across disciplines, to prior learning, and to non-
   academic learning experiences, including co-curricular learning, and opportunities to develop
   essential skills for delivering information through varied communication modes (written,
   spoken, digital) in a coherent manner appropriate for college success.
   ●   Connect with your advising team, which will guide you in setting goals, exploring educational
   and career options, and developing an individualized educational plan and guide students'
   exploration and collaboration across the contours of global diversity as manifested at
   LaGuardia Community College.

Attendance policy for all ELA department classes (participation grading required) –

Class attendance and participation are significant components of the learning process and play a
major role in determining overall student academic achievement. Therefore, students are strongly
encouraged to attend and participate in all class sessions of the courses in which they are
registered.

For Verification of Enrollment purposes faculty must record attendance for the first two (2)
weeks of classes. A student who does not attend any of the class meetings during the first two (2)
weeks (or the equivalent in online courses) will be reported as “Never Attended” and dropped
from the course.

Participation will be linked to completion of course activities and class assignments, which
include required class attendance or, for asynchronous courses, completing specific activities.
Please carefully review the course syllabus to determine how participation and attendance are
assessed in your class.

Please know that you are registered for an in-person class, and it is important to come to class
and actively engage in it. Excessive absences will impact your grade and good standing in this
class.

CUNY Policy on Academic Integrity

“Academic Dishonesty is prohibited in the City University of New York and is punishable by
penalties ranging from a grade of “F” on a given test, research paper or assignment, to an “F” in
the course, or suspension or expulsion from the College. Academic Dishonesty includes:

CHEATING — the unauthorized use or attempted use of material, information, notes, study aids,
devices or communication during an academic exercise. Examples: Copying from a student
during an examination, unauthorized collaboration on take home assignments, submitting
someone else’s work as your own, allowing another student to take an examination for you, or
unauthorized use of notes, electronic devices or other materials during an examination.

PLAGIARISM — the act of presenting another person’s ideas, research or writings as your own.
Examples: Copying another person’s actual words without the use of quotation marks and/or
footnotes, failure to acknowledge a source when using information that is not common
knowledge or failure to acknowledge collaborators on homework or laboratory assignments.

INTERNET PLAGIARISM — plagiarism that includes the submitting of downloaded term
papers or parts of term papers as a student’s own work, paraphrasing or copying information
from the internet without citing the source, as well as other forms of “cutting and pasting.

OBTAINING UNFAIR ADVANTAGE — any activity that intentionally or unintentionally
gives a student an unfair academic advantage over other students. Examples: Stealing, circulating
or otherwise gaining access to unauthorized examination materials, intentionally obstructing or
interfering with another student’s work, depriving other students of access to needed class or
library materials by stealing, destroying or defacing them, retaining or circulating examination
materials that clearly should have been returned at the end of the exam. FALSIFICATION

OF RECORDS AND OFFICIAL DOCUMENTS — Examples: Forging signatures or
authorization, falsifying information on academic records, falsifying official documents such as
grade reports, drop/add forms, ID cards or other college documents.

MISCONDUCT ON INTERNSHIPS — behavior inappropriate to a professional setting or in
violation of the rules established by either the College or the internship site. Noncompliance with
local, state and federal laws while on internship is also included. Above adapted from The
CUNY Policy on Academic Integrity, June 2004 (p. 201).”

*An important note of Academic Integrity

Please know that appropriation of the ideas of others is a serious matter. Plagiarism does not just
constitute quoting someone without offering reference, as if the idea were your own. Breach to
academic integrity is also committed when we:
   - copy word for word a text
   - paraphrase another without giving the other person credit
   - make our work available to a friend to “help” him or her write his or her paper
   - make use of a written paper of another student and paraphrase his or her work
   - use of your own original work from another class
   - quote the words of another as your own

Additionally, if it is the case that the ideas of another, be it of a fellow colleague in the
classroom, or of a particular essay or book chapter, have helped you develop a thought, always
give credit to the person and source. Should you have questions about academic integrity, feel
free to consult with me.

   *An important note on writing emails: Please be mindful when you write emails. Whenever
   you come to class and whenever you communicate with others in the College, be aware that you
   are in a professional environment. Similarly, whenever you write an email to your professor, you
   are writing a professional document. Make sure you always include a greeting (Hello Professor
   X, Hi Professor X, Good morning Professor X), followed by a well-written text (as
   grammatically correct as possible and formal).

   Please make sure to never send an attachment with an empty email. If your professor has allowed
   you to revise and resubmit an essay or an assignment of any kind, be sure to add a message to
   thank for the opportunity to revise and/or to submit late and remind the professor the assignment
   that you are attaching. Do not take for granted the opportunity and never send an empty email.
   This is disrespectful behavior and speaks badly of you.

   Keep in mind that emails written from your smart phone are not text messages; they are emails
   and should thus be written carefully and professionally. Lastly—depending on the nature of your
   message—be sure to say “Thank you,” or “Hope to hear from you soon,” or “Thank you for your
   time,” followed by your name.

   Writing emails is a common practice nowadays. As a pre-service teacher, you must be aware that
   professionalism is important, and it is part of your college education. You may have to write
   emails to professors, cooperating teachers, and principals. Please make sure that you present
   yourself, at all times, in a professional manner. Therefore, please make sure your email address
   follows suit: use your LaGuardia email, or if it is a gmail account (for instance) make sure your
   username is your name (not something in the likes of [redacted email]).

   How to be Successful in this   Course

1.   Check your LaGuardia email every day (and preferred means of   communication) to make sure you see
   everything provided to help you.
2.   Follow your assignment checklist (see last page of this syllabus) line by line!   Check items off the list as
   you complete them. [Make one for your other courses.]
3.   Make it a point to read all the information posted. It’s posted for a reason!
4.   Begin every chapter at the END of the chapter.   Read the summary. This will help you understand the
   reading content later for your other courses.

5.   Skim the chapter/material first to get an idea of what it’s about. This will help you gauge the level of
   difficulty and prioritize your time before you begin for your other courses.
6.   Make flashcards. In your own handwriting, on your own paper of choice. Spending time to write them out
   will help you to remember the keywords and concepts faster for your other courses.
7.   Keep a chronological notebook of YOUR NOTES in YOUR OWN WORDS. Use it to study and/or help
   you with research papers.
8.   Try to stay a few assignments ahead when possible. When you complete one day’s assignment, go ahead to
   the next day and do the next assignment. This will make your due dates seem a little shorter. (If you can stay
   an entire day or two ahead, that’d be even better because if something unexpected comes up, your activities
   will be done = no stress.)
9.   When you have trouble understanding certain concepts, try this: try to explain it to a classmate or me in
   writing or verbally or draw it out. You don’t have to be neat and use the lines of the paper – just scribble
   everywhere until you fill one side of it. Use the other side for the next concept.
10.  Another way to help you remember concepts: make post-it notes and stick them in places you pass several
   times a day so you see them repeatedly (even if it’s subconsciously).
11.  Take advantage of all additional resources in each module.
12.  Form a study group! Try to meet virtually or in person with each other on a weekly basis for study and peer
   support. Students who do so report greater progress and less stress.
13.  Take advantage of all extra credit assignments offered!
14.  Update your ePortfolio! Look at and comment on each other’s ePortfolios!
15.  Always communicate with me! An important part of being successful is to reach out and ask for assistance
   whenever needed. I am here for that! Also, research shows that a well-established relationship with your
   professor can do wonders. Everyone wins!
